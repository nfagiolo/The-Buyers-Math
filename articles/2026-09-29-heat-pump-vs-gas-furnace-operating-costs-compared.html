Heat Pump vs. Gas Furnace: Operating Costs Compared

Choosing between a heat pump and a natural gas furnace is one of the most consequential financial decisions a homeowner can make. While upfront equipment and installation expenses carry significant weight, ongoing operating costs ultimately determine the long-term affordability of your HVAC system. Comparing the day-to-day cost of running a heat pump versus a gas furnace is not a simple apples-to-apples comparison. It requires an understanding of energy efficiency ratings, regional utility pricing, and the thermodynamic realities of local winter weather.

Energy Efficiency Metrics: COP vs. AFUE

To evaluate fuel consumption, you must first understand how each system transforms raw energy into usable heat. The mechanisms are fundamentally different, leading to radically different efficiency ratings.

  • Gas Furnaces (AFUE): Furnaces generate heat via combustion. Their efficiency is measured by Annual Fuel Utilization Efficiency (AFUE). A standard-efficiency furnace operates around 80% AFUE, meaning 80% of the gas is converted to indoor heat while 20% escapes through the flue. High-efficiency condensing furnaces reach between 90% and 98% AFUE. Crucially, a gas furnace can never exceed 100% efficiency because it cannot generate more heat energy than the fuel itself contains.
  • Heat Pumps (COP and HSPF2): Heat pumps do not create heat; they extract thermal energy from outdoor air and transfer it indoors using refrigerant under pressure. Because they move existing heat, their efficiency is measured by the Coefficient of Performance (COP) or Heating Seasonal Performance Factor (HSPF2). A modern heat pump typically operates at a COP between 2.0 and 4.0 in moderate temperatures. A COP of 3.0 indicates that for every 1 unit of electrical energy consumed, the system delivers 3 units of thermal energy—effectively 300% efficiency.

The Math: Calculating Cost per Million BTUs (MMBtu)

Because natural gas is billed in therms (or CCF) and electricity is billed in kilowatt-hours (kWh), you must convert both fuels to a shared standard of heat output: one million British Thermal Units (MMBtu). One therm contains approximately 100,000 BTUs, while one kWh of electricity contains 3,412 BTUs.

The standard formulas to calculate the cost to produce 1 MMBtu of deliverable heat are:

  • Gas Furnace: (Cost per Therm / AFUE) × 10
  • Heat Pump: (Cost per kWh / (COP × 3,412)) × 1,000,000

For example, assume a national average natural gas price of $1.40 per therm and an electricity price of $0.16 per kWh, running a 95% AFUE furnace and a heat pump averaging a seasonal COP of 2.8:

  • 95% Efficient Furnace: ($1.40 / 0.95) × 10 = $14.74 per MMBtu
  • Heat Pump at 2.8 COP: ($0.16 / (2.8 × 3,412)) × 1,000,000 = $16.75 per MMBtu

In this baseline scenario, natural gas holds a slight operational cost advantage. However, adjusting either utility rate shifts the outcome drastically.

The Impact of Climate and Temperature on Operational Expense

Operating costs for a gas furnace remain remarkably consistent regardless of outdoor temperatures. Burning one therm of gas delivers roughly the same amount of heat whether the outside air is 45°F or -10°F.

Heat pumps, by contrast, exhibit dynamic operating costs directly tied to ambient outdoor temperatures:

  • Mild Weather (40°F to 60°F): Ambient air holds abundant thermal energy. Inverter-driven heat pumps operate at peak efficiency, reaching COPs of 3.5 to 4.5. In these conditions, electricity consumption drops dramatically, consistently outperforming gas furnaces on cost.
  • Freezing Weather (20°F to 32°F): Air-source units work harder to extract heat. Efficiency drops to a COP of roughly 2.0 to 2.5. Operating costs closely mirror high-efficiency gas furnaces in areas with balanced utility rates.
  • Extreme Cold (Below 10°F): Standard heat pumps experience sharp drops in capacity. If the unit relies on supplemental electric resistance heat strips (which run at an expensive COP of 1.0), heating bills can surge significantly. While modern cold-climate heat pumps (CCHPs) maintain high efficiency down to -15°F, prolonged deep freezes still tip operating cost advantages toward natural gas.

The "Spark Spread": How Regional Utility Rates Tip the Balance

The single greatest factor governing your heating bills is the regional ratio between electric rates and natural gas rates, commonly known in commercial energy as the "spark spread."

In regions like the Pacific Northwest, where hydroelectric infrastructure keeps electricity relatively inexpensive, heat pumps boast substantially lower operating costs than gas furnaces. Conversely, in regions with expensive electricity and access to low-cost regional shale gas (such as parts of the American Midwest and Northeast), a high-efficiency gas furnace remains cheaper to run throughout the winter.

Homeowners with existing rooftop solar installations represent a special case: generating your own electricity reduces marginal operating costs to near zero, making the heat pump vastly more economical than purchasing utility gas.

Maintenance and Ancillary Expenses

Routine operating costs extend beyond utility bills to annual maintenance and component wear. A gas furnace operates for roughly five months of the year, leaving cooling duties to a separate air conditioning unit. A heat pump handles both heating and cooling duties, running year-round. This continuous operation can lead to accelerated wear and slightly higher annual maintenance expenses over the lifespan of the equipment.

Strategic Verdict: Which System Minimizes Your Bills?

To determine the most cost-effective path for your home, evaluate your profile against these conditions:

  • A heat pump yields lower operating costs if: You live in a mild-to-moderate climate (USDA Zones 4–8), your local electricity rates are low relative to natural gas, or you generate on-site solar power.
  • A gas furnace yields lower operating costs if: You endure long sub-zero winters, pay exceptionally high electrical rates, or have access to inexpensive municipal natural gas.
  • A dual-fuel (hybrid) system offers the optimal compromise: By pairing an electric heat pump with a secondary gas furnace, an automated thermostat runs the heat pump during moderate weather and switches to the furnace only when freezing temperatures make gas the more economical fuel.
