How to Safely Size a Backup Generator for a Well Pump and AC

When severe weather knocks out the electrical grid, maintaining access to clean water and climate control becomes an immediate priority. A backup generator capable of powering both a deep well pump and a central air conditioning unit ensures that your household remains functional and safe. However, sizing a generator for these specific appliances is notoriously complex. Both devices rely on electric motors that draw significantly more power at startup than during standard operation. Undersizing a generator can cause voltage drops that permanently damage motor windings, trip circuit breakers, or stall the generator engine altogether.

Understanding Running Watts vs. Starting (Surge) Watts

To safely size a generator, you must differentiate between running (rated) watts and starting (surge) watts. Inductive loads—appliances driven by compressors and electric motors—require an intense burst of electrical current to break mechanical inertia and initiate rotation. This initial burst is known as inrush current or Locked Rotor Amps (LRA).

  • Running (Rated) Watts: The continuous electrical energy required to keep the motor operating under normal mechanical load once it reaches operating speed.
  • Starting (Surge) Watts: The momentary power spike required to overcome mechanical resistance, lasting from a fraction of a second to two full seconds. Surge power can be two to five times higher than running wattage.

Your generator must possess adequate continuous capacity to handle the combined running load of both appliances, alongside sufficient peak capacity to absorb the massive startup spike of whichever motor cycles on second.

Calculating Well Pump Power Demands

Most residential well pumps are submersible units operating on a dedicated 240-volt circuit. The power demand is directly correlated to the motor's horsepower (HP) rating and the depth of the well.

  • 1/2 HP Pump: Requires roughly 1,000 running watts and approximately 2,100 to 2,500 starting watts.
  • 3/4 HP Pump: Requires roughly 1,500 running watts and approximately 3,000 to 4,000 starting watts.
  • 1.5 HP Pump: Requires roughly 2,500 running watts and upwards of 5,000 to 6,000 starting watts.

Always inspect the electrical data plate on your pressure switch or controller box. To determine wattage manually, use the formula: Volts × Amps = Watts. For instance, a pump drawing 9.5 Running Load Amps (RLA) at 240 volts requires 2,280 running watts.

Calculating Central Air Conditioner Demands

Central air conditioning systems place the heaviest inductive demand on a residential electrical system. Sizing depends entirely on the unit's cooling capacity (measured in tons) and efficiency rating (SEER).

  • 2.5-Ton System: Approximately 3,000 to 3,500 running watts, with an unassisted starting surge between 10,000 and 14,000 watts.
  • 3.0-Ton System: Approximately 3,500 to 4,200 running watts, with an unassisted starting surge between 14,000 and 18,000 watts.
  • 4.0-Ton System: Approximately 4,800 to 5,500 running watts, with an unassisted starting surge exceeding 20,000 watts.

Look at the condenser unit’s exterior data plate for the LRA (Locked Rotor Amps) rating. Multiply the LRA by 240 volts to calculate the absolute peak wattage the compressor might pull when starting across the line without assistance.

The Soft-Start Advantage for Air Conditioners

Because central AC compressors demand massive surge wattage, attempting to supply raw across-the-line starting power often requires an oversized 20kW+ standby generator. Installing an aftermarket soft-starter kit on the AC condenser alters this equation. A soft starter electronically ramps up voltage across the motor windings over several milliseconds, reducing peak inrush current by 60% to 70%. Consequently, a 3-ton AC compressor that ordinarily spikes to 16,000 watts can start smoothly with a surge of only 5,000 to 6,000 watts, allowing you to install a significantly smaller, more fuel-efficient generator.

Step-by-Step Sizing Formula

To safely determine your required generator capacity, use the following engineering formula to account for sequential starting, rather than calculating worst-case simultaneous starts:

  • Step 1 (Combine Running Loads): Add the continuous running wattage of the well pump, the air conditioner, and any secondary critical loads (refrigeration, lighting, internet).
  • Step 2 (Determine Maximum Surge): Identify the single appliance with the highest starting surge. If the AC lacks a soft starter, this will be the AC. If the AC has a soft starter, the well pump and AC may have comparable surges.
  • Step 3 (Calculate Required Peak Capacity): Add the highest individual starting surge to the running wattages of all other actively running appliances.
  • Step 4 (Add a Safety Margin): Add a 20% safety buffer to protect the generator against thermal throttling and ensure it does not run continuously at 100% capacity.

Real-World Sizing Example

Consider a home with a 3/4 HP well pump (1,500W run / 3,500W surge) and a 3-ton AC equipped with a soft starter (3,800W run / 6,000W surge):

  • Total Running Load: 1,500W + 3,800W = 5,300 Watts
  • Largest Starting Surge: AC at 6,000 Watts
  • Peak Calculation: 6,000W (AC Surge) + 1,500W (Well Pump Running) = 7,500 Watts peak
  • Add 20% Margin: 7,500W × 1.20 = 9,000 Watts

In this scenario, a generator rated for 8,000 running watts and 10,000 starting watts safely handles the load. Without the soft starter, an 18,000-watt surge would demand a 20kW or larger generator.

Critical Installation and Safety Considerations

Safely powering these heavy loads involves more than raw generator capacity. You must comply with local electrical codes and the National Electrical Code (NEC):

  • Manual or Automatic Transfer Switch: Never connect a generator via a standard outlet or "suicide cord." A code-compliant transfer switch or mechanical interlock prevents lethal backfeeding into the utility grid while ensuring you do not overload individual branch circuits.
  • Clean Power (Total Harmonic Distortion): Well pump controllers and modern variable-speed HVAC control boards are sensitive to harmonic distortion. Choose a generator with less than 5% Total Harmonic Distortion (THD) to avoid frying sensitive microprocessor boards.
  • Fuel Source Derating: If your generator runs on Liquid Propane (LP) or Natural Gas (NG) instead of gasoline, derate the manufacturer's wattage specification by 10% to 20%, as gaseous fuels possess lower energy densities per unit volume.
