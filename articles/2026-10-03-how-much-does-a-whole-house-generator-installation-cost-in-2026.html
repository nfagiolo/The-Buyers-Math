How Much Does a Whole-House Generator Installation Cost in 2026?

Most homeowners will pay $10,000 to $22,000 for a professionally installed whole-house standby generator in 2026. Smaller 10-14 kW units can come in near $8,000, while 22-26 kW systems with a large transfer switch and long gas run often exceed $20,000. Premium 30-48 kW installations for large homes can reach $30,000 or more. These are typical national ranges; costs run 10-25% higher in high-labor-cost metros and after major storms, when installer demand spikes.

What Drives the Price

Generator Size

Size is the biggest variable. A 14-18 kW unit often handles essentials plus a central AC for an average home, while a 22-26 kW unit covers most 2,500-3,500 sq. ft. homes with few compromises. Oversizing wastes thousands; undersizing leaves you managing loads in an outage. Use our Backup Generator Sizing Guide to total your running and starting watts before you request quotes.

Fuel Type

Natural gas is cheaper to run and never needs refilling, but requires a utility line with adequate capacity. Propane adds a tank cost but works anywhere and stores well. Expect a natural gas hookup to cost $500-$2,000 for short runs, versus $2,000-$6,000 for propane with tank and piping.

Site Conditions

  • Distance to panel and gas meter: Each additional 25 feet of trenching and conduit can add $500-$1,500.
  • Setback requirements: Most codes require 5 feet from windows and openings, sometimes more, limiting placement.
  • Panel capacity: An older 100-amp panel may need a $1,500-$4,000 upgrade.
  • Terrain: Rock, slopes, or driveways to cross raise excavation costs.

Permits and Local Rules

Permit fees vary widely by municipality. Some areas also require noise-ordinance compliance, HOA approval, or a licensed gas fitter. Budget a few extra weeks for approvals and utility inspection.

Ongoing Costs to Budget

  • Annual maintenance: $200-$500 for oil, filters, spark plugs, and testing.
  • Extended warranty or service plan: $300-$800 per year.
  • Fuel: Roughly $1-$3 per hour on natural gas at half load; more on propane.
  • Insurance: Some insurers offer a modest discount for standby generators.

DIY vs. Professional Contractor

A whole-house standby generator is not a typical weekend project. It combines high-voltage electrical work, pressurized gas piping, and code compliance, where errors risk fire, carbon monoxide exposure, or backfeeding power onto utility lines and endangering line workers.

Where DIY Makes Sense

  • Researching and sizing the system
  • Clearing the site and, where allowed, preparing the pad
  • Handling routine upkeep like visual checks and battery tests

Always Hire a Pro For

  • Transfer switch and panel connections
  • Gas piping and pressure testing
  • Final commissioning and load testing

Get at least three written quotes, confirm licensing and insurance, and ask whether the installer is a certified dealer for the brand.

Ways to Lower the Total

  • Choose a transfer switch with load management instead of upsizing the generator.
  • Place the unit close to both the gas meter and electrical panel.
  • Schedule installation in the off-season (late winter or early spring).

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does installation take?

The physical work usually takes one to three days. The full process, including quotes, permitting, utility coordination, and equipment delivery, often takes 4-12 weeks, and longer after hurricane or storm season.

Does a whole-house generator increase home value?

Typically it recovers about 50-70% of its cost at resale, though this varies by region. It is especially attractive in areas with frequent outages, and can speed up sales in storm-prone markets.

What size generator do I need?

Most average homes need 14-26 kW. The right size depends on your central AC tonnage, electric appliances, well pump, and whether you want to power the whole home or just essentials. Calculate your loads first with the Backup Generator Sizing Guide, then have an electrician verify before purchase.
