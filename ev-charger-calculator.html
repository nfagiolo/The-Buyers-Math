Understanding Your EV Charger Installation Quote

Charging an EV at home on residential utility electric rates typically saves $1,000 to $1,800 per year compared to fueling an equivalent gasoline automobile. However, installing a continuous high-amperage 240V load demands strict adherence to the National Electrical Code (NEC).

4 EV Charger Contractor Red Flags to Watch For

  • Installing Cheap Builder-Grade NEMA 14-50 Outlets: Cheap $15 RV-style receptacles (such as Leviton 50A) are not designed for continuous 8-hour thermal loads and frequently overheat or melt. Electricians must install industrial-grade receptacles (such as Bryant or Hubbell) or hardwire the station directly.
  • Ignoring the NEC 80% Continuous Load Rule: EV charging is classified as a continuous load (drawing power for 3+ hours). A 48A charger requires a dedicated 60A breaker and #6 AWG copper wire. Installing a 48A charger on a 50A circuit violates electrical code and creates a serious fire hazard.
  • Failing to Pull a Municipal Electrical Permit: In almost all jurisdictions, adding a dedicated 240V circuit requires an electrical permit and final inspector sign-off. Unpermitted electrical work can jeopardize your homeowners insurance policy if an electrical fire occurs.
  • Quoting an Unnecessary $4,000 200A Service Upgrade: If your 100A or 150A panel is at capacity, an electrician can install an energy management load shedding device (such as a DCC-9 or smart splitter) for $800–$1,000, legally avoiding a full utility service drop heavy-up.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I charge an EV with a standard 120V household outlet?

Level 1 charging on a 120V wall outlet adds only 3 to 5 miles of range per hour, taking over 24 hours to recharge a depleted battery. A Level 2 240V station charges in 6 to 9 hours overnight.

How much does it cost to power an EV compared to gas?

At average US utility rates of $0.16/kWh, charging an EV costs roughly $0.04 to $0.05 per mile. A 28 MPG gasoline car at $3.50/gallon costs roughly $0.125 per mile—yielding over $1,000 in net annual savings for an average 12,000-mile driver.
